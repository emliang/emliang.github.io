Suppose we use a model to divide a one-unit budget between two services. A prediction of 0.51 units for each is close to the reference allocation of 0.5 each. Together, though, the two requests exceed the available budget. How can we learn useful allocations that we can actually carry out?

A robot route must clear obstacles; a generated molecule must obey structural rules. The budget example gives us a small version of the same problem. We will keep it throughout: first predict one allocation, then generate a collection of alternatives, and see what constraint handling changes in each case.

Accurate, yet impossible to use

Accuracy asks how close the allocation is to a reference. Feasibility asks whether it fits the budget. Utility asks how well it serves the two services. These can disagree: the prediction above is close to its reference but over budget; an allocation of zero to both services is feasible but may be of little use.

A neural network can learn from past solutions, taking demand and other features as inputs and proposing an allocation. The ordinary layers below explain how it computes a candidate. To ensure that the candidate fits the budget, we also need to identify where feasibility comes from: the predictor, its output representation, or an operation applied afterwards.

Draw the feasible set first

Let us make the budget rule explicit. Write the two allocations as y1y_1y1​ and y2y_2y2​. They must be nonnegative, and their sum must not exceed one. The allowed outputs form a triangle:

K={y:y1≥0,y2≥0,y1+y2≤1}.K=\left\{y:\begin{aligned}y_1&\ge0,\quad y_2\ge0,\\y_1+y_2&\le1\end{aligned}\right\}.K={y:y1​y1​+y2​​≥0,y2​≥0,≤1​}.

The point (0.8,0.8)(0.8,0.8)(0.8,0.8) sits outside this triangle. The points (0.4,0.4)(0.4,0.4)(0.4,0.4), (1,0)(1,0)(1,0), and (0,0)(0,0)(0,0) lie inside or on its boundary. Whether those feasible points are good allocations is a separate question.

More generally, a feasible set can combine equalities, such as conservation laws, with inequalities, such as capacity limits. It may depend on an input: today's demand, the current robot state, or a requested output schema. We can write this as KxK_xKx​, where xxx describes the instance. The important output is the final returned yyy, after any decoding, completion, correction, or solve.

A hard requirement decides which outputs are admissible; the algorithm must still enforce it. For the triangle, that means staying within all three inequalities. In a numerical implementation, finite precision also matters: feasibility is checked by comparing equality and inequality residuals with stated tolerances.

Our triangle is a nonempty convex set with linear inequalities, which makes the mechanisms easy to draw. Other tasks have surfaces, holes, separated regions, or discrete choices, as the atlas below shows. These differences matter: a construction that works for the triangle may not work for another geometry.

Can we just add a penalty?

One way to encourage the network to stay inside the triangle is to make violations expensive. Add a weighted constraint penalty to the task loss, so training sees both the desired allocation and the cost of breaking the rule. This also uses constraint knowledge when feasible labels are scarce. DL2, for example, translates logical specifications into losses that can be optimized with gradient methods.[1]

The penalty weight controls how strongly the optimizer resists a violation. Its effect depends on the model, data, optimization accuracy, and how violations are aggregated. In particular, a small average violation can conceal a few allocations that exceed the budget.

To isolate the idea, consider a pointwise quadratic-penalty problem. We want to stay near the preferred allocation ccc = (0.8,0.8)(0.8,0.8)(0.8,0.8), while penalizing an exceeded budget:

The persistent violation comes from this particular quadratic penalty; exact-penalty methods can recover feasible optima under suitable conditions. For a learned predictor, we still need to connect the training objective to the outputs it returns. That leads to the next question: can we enforce the rule while constructing the output, or after the network proposes it?

Four families of constraint handling

A penalty changes the incentive to produce a feasible output. Other methods change how the output is constructed or what happens to it afterwards. Our survey groups prediction methods into four families: training-based approaches, structured NN layers, constraint parameterization, and post-processing. The families can overlap: the same projection can be a trained layer or a correction applied afterwards.

Compare three ways to meet the budget: keep the candidate at (0.8, 0.8) and increase the penalty weight. Then switch to projection and feasible coordinates. Watch whether the output approaches the triangle, is moved onto it, or is constructed inside it.

The moving point exposes the difference: a larger quadratic penalty reduces the violation, whereas projection reaches the triangle. A structured projection layer and a post-processing projection have the same forward result here; only the former is included in training. With parameterization, the controls describe how to construct an allocation rather than where to move a candidate.

Training-based approaches: change the predictor

Constraints can shape the training loss, the learning problem, or a verification-guided modification of the model.[2][3] The resulting predictor runs directly at inference. The important question is whether the method improves average behavior or establishes a property for every input in a specified region.

Structured NN layers: train through an output operation

A layer can transform a prediction, complete missing variables, or solve a constrained optimization problem. The task loss sees its output, and training uses its derivative or a method-specific backward approximation, as in OptNet and differentiable convex optimization layers.[4][5] For our triangle, a projection layer returns the nearest feasible allocation and lets the predictor learn to anticipate the change. Feasibility comes from the forward operation and its numerical accuracy.

Constraint parameterization: use a feasible representation

Instead of repairing an allocation, we can change what the network predicts. It might produce coefficients that combine feasible generators, parameters of a distribution supported on feasible outputs, or coordinates for a feasible-set map. The representation then builds feasibility into output construction.

The triangle gives a coordinate-map example. Instead of predicting an allocation that may need repair, let the network choose two coordinates: bbb is the fraction of the budget used, and qqq is the share assigned to the first service. With both in [0,1][0,1][0,1], decode the allocation as (bq,b(1−q))\bigl(bq,b(1-q)\bigr)(bq,b(1−q)). Its entries are nonnegative and sum to b≤1b\le1b≤1, so the budget rule is built into the output.

For example, b=0.6b=0.6b=0.6 and q=0.7q=0.7q=0.7 give (0.42,0.18)(0.42,0.18)(0.42,0.18). These coordinates describe how to construct a feasible allocation; they are not an allocation awaiting correction. The explorer lets you vary them directly.

ConstraintNet illustrates feasible combinations: admissible weights combine the vertices of an input-dependent polytope;[6] gauge-based approaches map a bounded reference region into the feasible set, with equality completion where required.[7] For a feasible distribution, sampling and taking a mean are different operations: a mean of feasible points can lie outside a nonconvex set. Across representations, check both validity and coverage; restricting the outputs may exclude useful solutions.

Post-processing: recover the returned output

A correction, search, or solver can turn an earlier stage’s candidate into the decision that is actually used, without training through that operation. Projection is one option; geometry-specific recovery and solver warm starts are others.[8] The resulting decision may be feasible even when the raw prediction is not, but recovery takes time and can change its quality.

These families can work together. DC3, for example, combines equality completion with differentiable inequality correction.[9] A useful hybrid assigns complementary requirements to different operations, while checking that later stages preserve earlier constraints. The complete taxonomy and method-level details are in the prediction guide on GitHub.

From constrained prediction to constrained generation

So far, one input has led to one allocation. Now suppose the same demand admits several useful allocations: one favors the first service, another the second, and a third leaves some budget unused. We want to explore these alternatives before making a choice. A generative model uses random inputs to produce them, optionally conditioned on the same demand and task context.

The budget rule still applies to each sample. There is now another question: which allocations appear, and how often? A correction can make every sample feasible while concentrating most of them on the same few allocations. Generation therefore adds quality, diversity, and coverage to our evaluation, together with fidelity when the task specifies a target distribution.

Generators construct candidates in different ways. Direct generation produces a complete candidate, so the prediction mechanisms can be reused. This also includes generators distilled from iterative models into a faster noise-to-output map. Autoregressive generation builds candidates through successive choices. A grammar can restrict those choices,[10] but a locally valid prefix may still have no valid completion.

Diffusion and flow-based generation instead build samples through repeated updates. A diffusion sampler uses a learned denoiser or score to move through noise levels, with stochastic or deterministic updates. A flow-matching sampler integrates a learned velocity field from a prior toward a data distribution. We focus on these iterative models, where constraints can shape training, the sampling process, and the returned output.

Repeated updates give constraint methods several places to act: on an intermediate state, an estimate of the finished sample, or the final result. We will first locate those interventions, then return to the allocation triangle to see how they can change the distribution of feasible outputs.

Constraint handling in diffusion and flow models

Follow one allocation from noise to output. Each sampling update revises an unfinished proposal, so an intermediate state and the final allocation are different objects on which to impose a rule. For a robot planner, each update similarly revises the whole candidate route; generation time is separate from physical time along that route.

The survey follows four main viewpoints, then their combinations: training-based methods, geometry-aware sampling, constraint parameterization, and guidance, correction, and search. These viewpoints overlap; they describe how a method uses constraint information.

How do these viewpoints connect to actual methods? Reflected Diffusion and Riemannian Flow Matching illustrate geometry-aware sampling.[11][15] Universal Guidance steers sampling with an objective;[12] Projected Diffusion and Physics-Constrained Flow Matching apply corrections at different points.[13][14] Candidate or path search instead spends additional evaluations choosing among continuations, which is useful when an objective can be evaluated but not differentiated.

Combining mechanisms

Training can anticipate the correction used during sampling, or a pipeline can assign different requirements to different stages. Their compatibility matters: an output-space guidance step can undo feasibility supplied by a coordinate map. Guide within admissible coordinates or use a correction that handles the combined requirements. A hybrid earns its extra cost when it improves the quality, diversity, or rate of obtaining valid outputs. The complete classification, including discrete interventions, bridge and control formulations, is in the generation guide on GitHub.

Choose from the operations you have

For our uniform-allocation target, projection is valid but changes the desired distribution; rejection preserves it but spends proposals. Choosing a mechanism therefore starts with the required outcome: feasibility of every sample, a statistic across samples, or a property of the sampling process. Then ask which operations the problem makes available.

Include preparation costs: building a feasible representation may require vertex enumeration or a reference-point solve. A model used as a solver warm start should save time over a conventional initialization at the same stopping tolerance.

When the same constraints recur, training or preprocessing costs can be spread across many queries. When constraints change, online construction and enforcement matter more. The choice should survive an evaluation of the complete pipeline, which is where we turn next.

Evaluate the result you will actually use

After choosing a mechanism, evaluate the complete procedure that produces the decision or sample you will use. Four questions connect the method to that outcome:

  • Constraint satisfaction: Does the requirement apply to each output or to a population statistic? An expectation bound does not ensure every sample is feasible. For sample-level constraints, what fraction of final outputs meet the chosen tolerances? Report equality and inequality residuals, including their largest values. State whether the evidence comes from a construction, solver, certificate, probability statement, or evaluated samples.
  • Output quality: For prediction, report accuracy against reference outputs and the utility of the resulting decisions. For generation, examine quality, diversity, and coverage; assess fidelity to a target distribution when the task specifies one.
  • Computational cost: Count preprocessing and training where relevant, then measure the entire inference procedure. Include checks, correction, decoding, failed attempts, and solver work. For generation, cost per valid sample can reveal what raw sampling speed hides.
  • Applicable scope: Which constraint geometry, input region, oracle, solver condition, or discretization does the result require? If feasibility holds in continuous time, explain how the numerical updates preserve it.

Apply these questions to the returned object. If a model predicts an infeasible candidate and a solver repairs it, report the repaired decision's quality and the complete latency. If a relaxation is rounded, check the rounded output against the original rules. A feasibility-preserving component is useful only insofar as the later stages retain its property.

Our small budget problem leaves us with two distinct goals. For prediction, return a useful allocation that fits the budget. For generation, also produce the right collection of alternatives. A constraint mechanism should be judged by the decisions or samples it ultimately delivers, together with the cost of producing them.
