Suppose a model helps divide a one-unit budget between two services. It predicts 0.51 units for each, close to a reference allocation of 0.5 each. The prediction error is small, but the total exceeds the budget. Learning useful allocations means respecting that limit as well as predicting accurately.

Robot routes must clear obstacles; generated molecules must obey structural rules. The budget problem captures the same distinction between a plausible proposal and a usable output. It also extends naturally from predicting one allocation to generating several alternatives.

Accurate, yet impossible to use

Accuracy asks how close the allocation is to a reference. Feasibility asks whether it fits the budget. Utility asks how well it serves the two services. A near-reference allocation can exceed the budget; a zero allocation is feasible but may be of little use.

Can we just add a penalty?

A constraint penalty makes violations expensive during training. Added to the task loss, it can supply constraint information even when feasible labels are scarce. DL2, for example, translates logical specifications into losses optimized with gradient methods.[1]

The penalty weight sets the trade-off between task loss and violation. The result also depends on the model, data, optimization accuracy, and aggregation: a small average violation can conceal a few allocations that exceed the budget.

Four families of constraint handling

Constraint handling can act during training, within a network layer, through the output representation, or after prediction. Our survey organizes these roles into four families: training-based approaches, structured NN layers, constraint parameterization, and post-processing.

For the triangle, choose two coordinates: bbb is the fraction of the budget used, and qqq is the share assigned to the first service. With both in [0,1][0,1][0,1], decode the allocation as (bq,b(1−q))\bigl(bq,b(1-q)\bigr)(bq,b(1−q)). Its entries are nonnegative and sum to b≤1b\le1b≤1, so the budget rule is built into the output.

Training-based approaches: change the predictor

Constraints can shape the training loss, the learning problem, or a verification-guided modification of the model.[2][3] The resulting predictor runs directly at inference. Improving average behavior and establishing a property for every input in a specified region are different outcomes.

Structured NN layers: train through an output operation

A layer can transform a prediction, complete missing variables, or solve a constrained optimization problem. The task loss is evaluated on the layer’s output; training uses its derivative or a method-specific backward approximation. Examples include OptNet and differentiable convex optimization layers.[4][5] The forward operation supplies feasibility up to its numerical accuracy. Training through it lets the predictor anticipate its effect.

Constraint parameterization: use a feasible representation

The budget decoder builds feasibility into the representation. Other representations use coefficients that combine feasible generators, parameters of a distribution supported on feasible outputs, or coordinates for a feasible-set map.

ConstraintNet illustrates feasible combinations: admissible weights combine the vertices of an input-dependent polytope;[6] gauge-based approaches map a bounded reference region into the feasible set, with equality completion where required.[7] The final operation still matters: a mean of feasible samples can lie outside a nonconvex set. Coverage matters too; a valid representation may exclude useful solutions.

Post-processing: recover the returned output

A correction, search, or solver recovers a decision from the candidate without training through that operation. Options include projection, geometry-specific recovery, and using the candidate to warm-start a solver.[8] Recovery can make an infeasible prediction usable, at a computational cost and with a possible change in decision quality.

These families can work together. DC3, for example, combines equality completion with differentiable inequality correction.[9] The final output must satisfy the combined requirements; later operations can undo constraints enforced earlier. Method-level details are in the prediction guide on GitHub.

From constrained prediction to constrained generation

The same demand may admit several useful allocations: one favors the first service, another the second, and a third leaves some budget unused. Rather than commit to one prediction, a generative model uses random inputs to propose alternatives, optionally conditioned on that demand and task context.

Each sample must still fit the budget. We also care about which allocations appear, and how often. A correction can make every sample feasible while concentrating them on a few allocations. Generation therefore requires attention to diversity and coverage as well as sample quality, and to fidelity when the task specifies a target distribution.

Direct generation produces a complete candidate, so prediction mechanisms can be reused. This includes iterative models distilled into a faster noise-to-output map. Autoregressive generation builds candidates through successive choices. A grammar can restrict those choices,[10] but a locally valid prefix may still have no valid completion.

Diffusion and flow-based generation instead build samples through repeated updates. A diffusion sampler uses a learned denoiser or score to move through noise levels, with stochastic or deterministic updates. A flow-matching sampler integrates a learned velocity field from a prior toward a data distribution. These iterative models are the focus here. Repeated updates create several places to impose constraints: the current state, an estimate of the completed sample, or the final output. These objects need not satisfy the same requirements.

Constraint handling in diffusion and flow models

For an iterative allocation generator, the budget rule applies to the completed allocation; noisy intermediate states need not satisfy it. In trajectory generation, each update similarly revises a candidate route. Generation time describes the sampler’s progress, distinct from physical time along the route.

For diffusion and flow models, our survey groups constraint handling into four overlapping viewpoints: training-based methods, geometry-aware sampling, constraint parameterization, and guidance, correction, and search.

Geometry-aware examples include Reflected Diffusion and Riemannian Flow Matching.[11][15] Universal Guidance steers sampling with an objective;[12] Projected Diffusion and Physics-Constrained Flow Matching apply corrections at different points.[13][14] Search instead evaluates and selects continuations, including when the objective cannot be differentiated.

Combining mechanisms

Training can anticipate a sampling correction, while different stages handle complementary requirements. Their order matters: output-space guidance can move a decoded sample outside the feasible set. Guiding within admissible coordinates or correcting for the combined requirements can avoid this conflict. The generation guide on GitHub covers the complete classification, including discrete interventions, bridge and control formulations.

Choose from the operations you have

In the triangle example, projection ensures feasibility but changes the target distribution; rejection preserves that distribution but discards proposals. The choice depends on the required outcome—feasibility of each sample, a population statistic, or a sampling-process property—and on the operations available for the task.

Include preparation costs: building a feasible representation may require vertex enumeration or a reference-point solve. A model used as a solver warm start should save time over a conventional initialization at the same stopping tolerance.

When the same constraints recur, training or preprocessing costs can be spread across many queries. When constraints change, online construction and enforcement matter more.

Evaluate the result you will actually use

Evaluate the complete procedure, including any decoding or repair, along four dimensions:

  • Constraint satisfaction: Distinguish requirements on each output from population statistics: an expectation bound does not ensure every sample is feasible. Report the fraction of outputs meeting the chosen tolerances and the equality and inequality residuals, including their largest values. Identify the evidence: a construction, solver, certificate, probability statement, or evaluated samples.
  • Output quality: For prediction, report accuracy against reference outputs and the utility of the resulting decisions. For generation, examine quality, diversity, and coverage; assess fidelity to a target distribution when the task specifies one.
  • Computational cost: Count preprocessing and training where relevant, then measure the entire inference procedure. Include checks, correction, decoding, failed attempts, and solver work. For generation, report cost per valid sample.
  • Applicable scope: Which constraint geometry, input region, oracle, solver condition, or discretization does the result require? If feasibility holds in continuous time, explain how the numerical updates preserve it.

If a solver repairs a prediction, measure the repaired decision’s quality and the complete latency. If a relaxation is rounded, check the rounded output against the original constraints. Later stages can invalidate an earlier feasibility guarantee.

Prediction asks for a useful allocation that fits the budget. Generation also asks for the right collection of alternatives. Feasibility, quality, distribution, and cost belong in the same comparison.
